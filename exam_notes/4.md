## CORE DEFINITIONS


### What is “coordination” in MAS?
Coordination is about getting multiple agents to make compatible decisions so the group can function effectively despite distributed information and control.[1]
In the course, coordination is framed through group decision mechanisms: leader election, voting, auctions, and bargaining, which typically rely on agents having preferences or valuations.[1]


### Cooperation vs collaboration
**Cooperation** (“to work with”): no defined shared goal, no shared ownership of the full operation, and only potentially/partially planned.[1]
**Collaboration** (“together + work”): shared goal, shared ownership, planned, and potentially includes central control/leader.[1]


### When do we need preferences/valuations?
Group decisions typically require agents to express either (a) ordinal preferences (rankings) as in voting, or (b) cardinal valuations (numbers like costs/utilities/bids) as in auctions and bargaining.[1]

## LEADER ELECTION


### Bully algorithm (message types)
The Bully algorithm uses three message types: **Election**, **Answer (Alive)**, and **Coordinator/Victory**.[1]


### Bully algorithm (core rules/flow)
- If an agent receives a Victory/Coordinator message, it treats the sender as coordinator.[1]
- If an agent has the highest ID, it broadcasts Victory/Coordinator (e.g., after recovery or when the coordinator fails).[1]
- If an agent receives an Election message from a higher ID, it waits (does nothing).[1]
- If an agent receives an Election message from a lower ID, it sends Answer and initiates its own Election.[1]
- If no one responds to an agent’s Election message, it claims Victory.[1]


### Problems/assumptions of Bully
Known issues highlighted in the slides are: all agents must be honest, new agents with higher IDs might never become leader, and the leader is chosen purely by ID.[1]
This means Bully is simple and deterministic, but it does not optimize “best leader” quality unless ID correlates with quality.[1]


### “Appointer + metrics” leader election (conceptual)
The slides present a scheme where leader election runs continuously in the background and selects a unique **Appointer**.[1]
The Appointer then requests health metrics from all agents (via convergecast), chooses the best leader, and informs everyone (broadcast), repeating periodically as a failsafe.[1]
The rationale is to decouple (i) finding a unique decision-maker from (ii) picking the best leader, turning leader selection into a centralized step performed by the appointer.[1]


### MST / Yo-Yo (how to select the Appointer)
A distributed Minimum Spanning Tree approach (Gallager–Humblet–Spira / “Yo-Yo” in the slides) is suggested as a way to construct a rooted structure that can be used to select a unique appointer and support broadcast/convergecast efficiently.[2][1]

## VOTING (SOCIAL CHOICE)


### What is voting?
Voting is making a group decision based on individual preferences.[1]
The lecture focuses on plurality voting, sequential majority elections, Borda count, and Slater ranking as representative methods.[1]


### Plurality voting (first-past-the-post)
Plurality voting is winner-takes-all: each agent votes for a single option, and the option with most votes wins.[1]
It can create “lost votes,” because votes for non-winners do not affect the final choice.[1]


### Majority runoff election
In majority runoff, the top two options enter a second round, and agents who supported losing options must pick among the two finalists.[1]
The slides note it can lead to tactical voting in the first round.[1]


### Sequential majority voting + Condorcet winner
Sequential majority voting runs a sequence of pairwise votes; the current winner faces the next option, and the final survivor is selected.[1]
The slides emphasize that the agenda/order can determine the outcome, and define a Condorcet winner as an option that wins regardless of sequence.[1]

## AUCTIONS (TASK/RESOURCE ALLOCATION)


### What is an auction (MAS interpretation)?
Auctions allocate tasks/resources among agents when each agent can express a valuation/bid for outcomes.[1]
They are especially useful when valuations are private and the system needs a decentralized allocation mechanism.[1]


### Common auction types (what to be able to define)
- **English auction**: an open, ascending-price auction where bids increase until no one is willing to bid higher.[3]
- **Dutch auction**: a descending-price auction where the price starts high and decreases until the first bidder accepts, and that bidder wins.[4]
- **First-price sealed-bid**: each bidder submits one hidden bid; the highest bidder wins and pays its own bid (common effect: bid shading).[5][6]
- **Vickrey (second-price sealed-bid)**: the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid.[7]


### Why is Vickrey “truth-revealing”?
The course slide explains that bidding your true valuation is a dominant strategy because overbidding can make you win and pay more than your value (negative surplus), while underbidding can make you lose when you would have gained positive surplus.[1]
Standard proofs formalize this by showing truthful bidding weakly dominates underbidding and overbidding in a second-price auction.[7]

## BARGAINING / NEGOTIATION


### What is bargaining?
Bargaining (negotiation) is used to agree on task/resource allocation and division.[1]
The lecture breaks it into: negotiation set (possible proposals), protocol (allowed interaction), strategies (what to propose), and rules (what to accept/reject).[1]


### Settings (who negotiates with whom)
The slides distinguish one-to-one, many-to-one (auction-like), and many-to-many negotiation (can be seen as \(n(n-1)/2\) parallel one-to-one negotiations).[1]


### Patient vs impatient agents (impasse intuition)
Patient agents have no incentive to change and may reject any other offer, causing impasse.[1]
Impatient agents adjust over time (concede or accept) and are required to reliably strike a deal.[1]


### Alternating offers + classical strategies
The alternating offers protocol has agents take turns making proposals; the other accepts or rejects, repeating until agreement/end.[1]
Classical strategies include **conceder** (concede early/fast) and **boulware** (hold firm early, concede late).[1]

### Q4: Explain Bully in 30–45 seconds (core steps).
**Answer:**

### Q10: Explain sequential majority voting and the “agenda” problem.
**Answer:**  
Sequential majority voting runs pairwise votes sequentially; the current winner continues against the next option until one remains.[1]
The lecture highlights that the sequence/order of pairwise comparisons can determine the winner.[1]


***


### Q11: What is a Condorcet winner (in this lecture’s framing)?
**Answer:**  
A Condorcet winner is an option that wins regardless of the vote sequence in sequential majority voting.[1]

### Q12: Define English and Dutch auctions.
**Answer:**  
An English auction is an open ascending auction where bids increase until no one bids higher.[3]
A Dutch auction is a descending-price auction where the price drops until the first bidder accepts, and that bidder wins.[4]

### Q13: Why is Vickrey auction truth-revealing?
**Answer:**  
The lecture’s payoff argument shows bidding above true value can lead to negative surplus, while bidding below can cause you to lose profitable wins, so truthful bidding is dominant.[1]
This is the standard dominance-of-truthful-bidding result for second-price auctions.[7]


***


### Q14: What is bargaining, and what are its four components?
**Answer:**  
Bargaining is negotiation to agree on task/resource allocation and division.[1]
It consists of a negotiation set, protocol, strategies, and acceptance/rejection rules.[1]


***


### Q15: What causes impasse in bargaining (patient vs impatient)?
**Answer:**  
Patient agents may reject any alternative and never change their offer, so negotiation can get stuck in impasse.[1]
Impatient agents adjust over time (concede/accept), which is needed to reach agreement.[1]
