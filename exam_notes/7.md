## CORE DEFINITIONS

### What is Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)?
MARL extends single-agent RL to settings where multiple agents learn simultaneously in a shared environment, creating non-stationary dynamics (each agent's policy changes affect others' transition/reward functions) and credit assignment challenges (who caused success/failure).

**Key challenges:**
- **Non-stationarity:** Other agents' policies change, so environment dynamics change from any single agent's perspective
- **Credit assignment:** Hard to determine which agent(s) caused outcomes in joint actions
- **Coordination without communication:** Agents must learn to cooperate/coordinate using only reward signals and observations

### What is Regret Matching?
Regret matching is an online learning algorithm from game theory where agents maintain cumulative regrets for not having played alternative actions, then sample future actions with probability proportional to positive regret.[1][2]

**Core mechanics:**
- Track counterfactual regret: "how much better would action \(a\) have been than what I did?"
- Actions with higher positive regret become more likely in future
- Actions with negative/zero regret are not selected (regret = 0 in probability calculation)
- Over time, time-average behavior converges toward correlated equilibrium in repeated games[2]

### What is Level-Based Foraging (LBF)?
A multi-agent benchmark environment where agents must coordinate to collect food items/reward patches distributed in space, explicitly designed to test MARL coordination, sparse rewards, and credit assignment.[3][4][5]

## REGRET MATCHING ALGORITHM FOUNDATIONS

### Formal algorithm (discrete actions)
For agent with action set \(A\), at time \(t\):[1][2]

1. **Instant regret** for action \(a\): 
   \[
   r_t(a) = u_t(a) - u_t(a_{taken})
   \]
   where \(u_t(a)\) is the payoff agent would have gotten with action \(a\), and \(a_{taken}\) is what was actually played[1]

2. **Cumulative regret**: 
   \[
   R_t(a) = \sum_{\tau=1}^{t} r_\tau(a)
   \]
[1]

3. **Action probability** at \(t+1\): 
   \[
   \pi_{t+1}(a) \propto \max(R_t(a), 0)
   \]
   If all regrets \(\le 0\), use uniform random[1]

### Equilibrium concept
Regret matching is known for converging in time-average to **correlated equilibrium** in repeated games under appropriate conditions (full feedback, stationary opponents).  Correlated equilibrium is weaker than Nash equilibrium but still captures coordination—agents receive correlated signals about what to do, and following those signals is in their interest.[2][1]

**Convergence meaning:**
- Time-average (not last-iterate): average policy over all timesteps approaches equilibrium
- Last-iterate convergence is not guaranteed in general games[2]
- Requires ability to compute counterfactual payoffs for actions not taken[1]

### Q1: What is the main idea of regret matching in a MARL setting?
**Answer:**
Regret matching tracks cumulative regrets for not having chosen alternative actions in the past, then selects future actions with probability proportional to positive regret. This encourages exploration of actions that would have historically performed better. In MARL, this provides a decentralized way for agents to learn coordination without explicit communication—each agent independently updates regrets based on observed rewards, and collective behavior emerges from these individual learning dynamics.[2][1][3]

### Q6: What equilibrium concept is regret matching associated with, and what does convergence mean operationally?
**Answer:**
Regret matching converges in time-average to correlated equilibrium in repeated games. Operationally, "time-average convergence" means the average policy over all timesteps approaches equilibrium, though last-iterate behavior may still fluctuate. Correlated equilibrium is weaker than Nash—agents receive correlated signals (in this case, their history-dependent regrets) and following those signals is in their interest. Last-iterate convergence (final policy being equilibrium) is not guaranteed in general games.[2][1]

### Q7: How do you compute counterfactual regret when you only executed one action per step?
**Answer:**
In my implementation, I compute instant regret by comparing the observed reward from the action taken to hypothetical rewards for alternative actions. Since we discretized state and actions, at each timestep I record the reward received and estimate what reward each alternative action would have yielded (using the reward function, e.g., distance shaping for hypothetical moves). This gives \(r_t(a) = u_t(a) - u_t(a_{taken})\) for each action \(a\). Cumulative regret \(R_t(a)\) sums these over time, and positive regrets determine next action probabilities.[1][3]

### Q8: How does adding ε-greedy change the interpretation of regret matching and its guarantees?
**Answer:**
Pure regret matching has theoretical convergence guarantees to correlated equilibrium under specific conditions (full feedback, stationary opponents). Adding ε-greedy introduces forced random exploration, which helps discovery in sparse-reward environments but breaks the pure regret-matching update rule—agents sometimes take actions not because of regret but because of random chance. This makes it an "exploration-augmented" variant: better empirical performance in MARL (handles partial observability, non-stationarity) but weaker theoretical guarantees. The algorithm is now a hybrid rather than textbook regret matching.[1][3]

### Q9: Why can reward shaping improve learning speed but also bias learned behavior?
**Answer:**
Reward shaping adds dense intermediate signals (e.g., "getting closer to reward is good") that guide agents faster than waiting for sparse terminal rewards. This speeds convergence. However, if shaping is poorly designed, agents may optimize the shaped reward instead of the true objective (reward hacking)—for example, circling near a patch to maintain low distance without collecting it. The key is ensuring the shaped reward is a potential-based shaping that preserves optimal policy, or carefully validating that learned behavior achieves the true goal (patch collection, not just proximity).[3]

### Q10: Is your global ledger a form of centralized training signal, and what would be a fully decentralized alternative?
**Answer:**
Yes, the global ledger is centralized bookkeeping: a central oracle tracks which patches are collected and enforces "reward once per patch." Learning itself is decentralized (each agent updates its own regrets locally), but the reward function has central knowledge. A fully decentralized alternative would be: (1) each agent maintains its own belief about collected patches (risks inconsistency, duplicate rewards); or (2) patches physically disappear or change appearance when collected, providing an environmental cue all agents can observe (stigmergy). The second option is more robust and closer to true decentralization.[3]

### Q12: Explain why collision avoidance is not just "safety," but also affects learning stability and sample efficiency.
**Answer:**
Collision avoidance (TTC + escape turns) changes the MDP the learner experiences. Without it, agents frequently collide, get stuck in deadlocks, and waste steps in unproductive states, which adds noise to learning signals (rewards become dominated by collision penalties rather than task progress). With effective collision avoidance, transitions are more predictable, agents spend more time in task-relevant states (exploring patches rather than resolving collisions), and sample efficiency improves because experiences are informative about the foraging task, not collision recovery. This is especially critical in dense scenarios (20 agents), where congestion could otherwise overwhelm learning.[7][3]

### Q14: If the map were tighter/more cluttered, what failure modes would appear first?
**Answer:**
In a tighter map with more obstacles:
1. **TTC deadlocks:** Collision avoidance may fail if agents cannot find escape routes (hysteresis lock keeps turning, but no safe direction exists)—agents get trapped
2. **Exploration collapse:** Discrete grid aliasing worsens in tight spaces (more states map to same cell), making regret signals noisier and slower to converge

### Q15: What does "non-stationarity" mean in MARL and how does it affect regret matching?
**Answer:**
Non-stationarity means that from any single agent's perspective, the environment dynamics are changing because other agents are learning and changing their policies. In Level-Based Foraging, this means: where other agents go affects which patches you can reach, your distance-shaping rewards, and collision-avoidance triggers. For regret matching, non-stationarity violates the "stationary opponent" assumption in convergence proofs—other agents aren't fixed strategies, they're learning too. In practice, this can slow or prevent convergence to equilibrium, or cause cycles. Your ε-greedy exploration helps by maintaining stochasticity, and reward shaping provides denser signals that help agents adapt to others' behavior changes.[4][2][3]

### Q16: Why is credit assignment hard in multi-agent foraging, and how does your system address it?
**Answer:**
Credit assignment is hard because: in a team task, success (collecting a patch) may result from joint effort (one agent explored left, another right, one actually collected), but rewards are typically given only to the agent that triggered the event. In my system, only the collecting agent gets the patch reward (via global ledger), which can be unfair. I address this partially with distance-shaping rewards: all agents get positive shaping for reducing distance to nearest patch, so even non-collectors receive some learning signal. However, this doesn't fully solve credit assignment—agents that "helped" by exploring elsewhere don't get explicit credit. A better solution might be team reward (all agents share collection reward) or counterfactual value estimation (how much did each agent contribute).[4][3]

### Q17: Describe the role of stigmergy in your MARL system.
**Answer:**
Stigmergy is indirect coordination via environmental cues—agents leave information in the environment that others perceive and react to. In my system, stigmergy is implicit: patches act as attractors (via distance shaping), and once collected (ledger enforced), they no longer attract agents. Agents indirectly coordinate by "sensing" that certain patches are gone (through lack of shaping reward or explicit observation if patches visually disappear). Additionally, other agents' positions affect collision-avoidance behavior (TTC detects others via LiDAR), which is a form of environmental sensing. This is weaker stigmergy than ant pheromones (no persistent environmental modification), but functionally similar—coordination emerges from sensing environment state rather than direct messages.[3]

### Q18: What are the key differences between your MARL approach and single-agent RL?
**Answer:**
Key differences:
1. **Non-stationarity:** In single-agent RL, environment dynamics are fixed. In MARL, other agents' changing policies make dynamics non-stationary from each agent's view.
2. **Credit assignment:** In single-agent, all reward/penalty is due to agent's own actions. In MARL, outcomes depend on joint actions, so credit is ambiguous.
3. **Coordination needs:** Single agent optimizes alone. MARL requires agents to learn complementary or cooperative behaviors (e.g., spreading out to cover patches).
4. **Scalability:** More agents = exponential joint action space if treated as single big agent; MARL treats agents independently with local updates, scaling better.
5. **Equilibrium concept:** Single-agent seeks optimal policy; MARL seeks equilibrium (Nash, correlated) where agents' policies are mutually best responses.

My system uses decentralized regret matching (each agent updates independently) to handle non-stationarity and scale, with reward shaping and ledger to ease credit assignment.[4][2][3]

### Q20: What would you change in your implementation to improve fairness and efficiency?
**Answer:**
To improve fairness and efficiency:

### Q21: Explain the TTC collision avoidance mechanism and why hysteresis lock is important.
**Answer:**
Time-To-Collision (TTC) estimates how soon a collision will occur given current velocities and positions (typically via LiDAR detecting obstacles/other agents). When TTC drops below a threshold (e.g., 2 seconds), the system limits forward speed and, if TTC is critically low, triggers an "escape turn" (rotate away from threat). Hysteresis lock means once the agent starts an escape turn, it commits to turning until TTC is safely above threshold—it doesn't immediately switch back to forward motion the instant TTC rises slightly. This prevents oscillation (turn, TTC rises, stop turn, TTC drops, repeat), which would cause jittery behavior and potential deadlock. Hysteresis stabilizes the control loop and ensures agents fully clear the collision risk before resuming normal operation.[7][3]

### Q22: What is correlated equilibrium and why is it relevant to regret matching?
**Answer:**
Correlated equilibrium is a game-theoretic solution concept weaker than Nash equilibrium. In correlated equilibrium, a mediator (or correlation device) sends each player a private signal recommending an action, and following that recommendation is in each player's interest given what others will do (assuming they also follow their signals). It captures coordination possibilities beyond independent randomization (Nash mixed strategies). Regret matching is relevant because its time-average behavior provably converges to correlated equilibrium in repeated games with full feedback. In MARL, this means regret-matching agents can learn to coordinate (correlate their actions in beneficial ways) without explicit communication—the "signals" are their own regret histories shaped by past interactions.[2][1]

### Q23: How does reward shaping via distance to nearest patch guide agent behavior?
**Answer:**
Distance-to-nearest-patch shaping gives a small positive reward when the agent reduces its distance to the closest uncollected patch, and a small penalty when distance increases. This creates a potential field or gradient toward patches, guiding agents via dense feedback rather than waiting for sparse patch-collection events. Behaviorally, agents learn that "moving toward patches" actions accumulate positive regret (they yield better shaping rewards), so those actions become more probable. Over time, this biases exploration toward productive regions of the environment (near patches) and speeds convergence compared to blind random search. The risk is agents might optimize proximity without collection if shaping overwhelms collection reward—hence balancing shaping magnitude with primary task reward is critical.[3]

### Q24: What are the trade-offs between direct communication and stigmergic coordination in MARL?

**Direct communication (messages between agents):**
- **Pros:** Explicit, fast information sharing; can coordinate complex plans; agents can negotiate or request help
- **Cons:** Requires communication infrastructure; bandwidth/latency limits; agents must agree on message semantics; scales poorly with many agents (message explosion)

**Stigmergic coordination (environmental cues):**
- **Pros:** Robust (no message loss), scalable (environment is shared "blackboard"), no direct agent-to-agent links needed, works in sparse networks
- **Cons:** Indirect, slower adaptation (must sense changes in environment); limited expressiveness (environment must encode information); requires environment modification capability

In my Level-Based Foraging setup, stigmergy via patches (attractors that disappear when collected) and collision cues (LiDAR detections) provides coordination without explicit messages, which is scalable and robust. Adding lightweight direct communication (e.g., "I'm claiming patch X") could improve efficiency without overwhelming the system.[3]

### Q25: How do you interpret "self-assigned territories" emerging without communication?
**Answer:**
"Self-assigned territories" means agents implicitly partition the environment via learning dynamics, not negotiation. This emerges because: (1) distance-shaping rewards guide each agent toward the nearest uncollected patch; (2) once an agent is close to a patch, its regret for "move toward that patch" grows, locking in that direction; (3) collision avoidance and spatial distribution mean different agents start in different areas and naturally converge on different patches. Over time, each agent's policy specializes to a region/set of patches. This is an emergent phenomenon of regret matching + reward shaping + spatial constraints—no explicit coordination, yet division of labor appears. It's analogous to animal territoriality emerging from local interactions without central planning.[3]

## RAPID-FIRE DEFINITIONS (30 seconds each)

**Regret matching:** Online learning where action probabilities increase for actions with positive cumulative regret (would have been better historically); converges to correlated equilibrium in repeated games.[2][1]

**Non-stationarity in MARL:** Environment dynamics change from each agent's view because other agents' policies are learning/changing, violating single-agent RL's fixed-dynamics assumption.[4][3]

**Credit assignment in MARL:** Difficulty determining which agent(s) caused a joint outcome (success/failure) when rewards are sparse and actions are concurrent.[4][3]

**TTC (Time-To-Collision):** Predictive metric estimating time until collision given current velocities/positions; used to trigger preemptive speed reduction or escape maneuvers.[7][3]

**Global reward ledger:** Centralized tracking of which patches collected, enforcing "reward once per patch" to eliminate duplicate credits and stabilize learning.[3]

**Correlated equilibrium:** Solution concept where a mediator sends correlated action recommendations to players, and following recommendations is in each player's interest; weaker than Nash, captures coordination.[2]

**ε-greedy exploration:** With probability ε, choose random action (explore); otherwise exploit current policy; ε often decays over time from high (explore early) to low (exploit learned policy).[3]

**Reward shaping:** Adding dense intermediate rewards (e.g., distance to goal) to sparse task rewards to guide learning; speeds convergence but risks biasing learned behavior if poorly designed.[3]

**Stigmergy:** Indirect coordination via environmental cues—agents modify or sense environment (e.g., pheromones, patch states), enabling coordination without direct messages.[3]

**Hysteresis lock:** Control mechanism that commits to a state/action until a threshold is clearly exceeded in the opposite direction, preventing rapid oscillation or jitter.[7][3]
